Portland, Oregon

Portland is a city that reveals itself slowly. A morning might begin beneath a ceiling of gray clouds, with wet pavement reflecting storefront lights and the smell of fresh coffee drifting from a neighborhood café. By afternoon, the clouds can lift over the West Hills, bicycles begin filling the streets, and the Willamette River catches a strip of silver between bridges. Beyond the city, evergreen forests, the Columbia River Gorge, Mount Hood, and the Oregon Coast remind you that Portland’s urban life is inseparable from the landscape around it.

The city’s history is older and more complicated than its relaxed contemporary reputation suggests. The Portland area has been home to Indigenous peoples for thousands of years, particularly communities connected to the Columbia and Willamette river systems. The city was incorporated in 1851 after emerging from a settlement that had become known as “Stumptown,” and its name itself was decided by a famous 1845 coin toss between founders Asa Lovejoy and Francis Pettygrove. Portland later grew as a major river and railroad city, with waves of Chinese, Japanese, European, African American, and other communities shaping its neighborhoods and institutions.

Today, Portland feels less like a single downtown surrounded by suburbs and more like a collection of distinct neighborhoods stitched together by bridges, bike lanes, transit lines, parks, and the river. Northwest streets climb toward Forest Park; Southeast Portland moves through old residential blocks, restaurants, gardens, and independent shops; Alberta, Mississippi, Hawthorne, Sellwood, and other districts each have their own rhythm. The city’s personality is often found in these ordinary details—a bookstore open late, a food cart tucked into an unassuming lot, a rainy afternoon spent over coffee, or a neighborhood park full of dogs and children.
