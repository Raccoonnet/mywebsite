Seattle, Washington

Seattle is a city of edges. Water wraps around it, hills rise and fall beneath its neighborhoods, ferries cross Elliott Bay, and on a clear day the mountains seem close enough to reach. The city occupies the eastern shore of Puget Sound between Lake Washington and the saltwater, with nearly 148 miles of freshwater shoreline and more than 53 miles of saltwater shoreline. That geography is not background scenery—it shapes how Seattle residents move, work, eat, spend weekends, and understand their city.

The modern city grew from a settlement established by the Denny Party at Alki Point in 1851, but Seattle’s story begins much earlier with the Coast Salish peoples who have lived on and cared for these lands and waterways for generations. The settlers moved across Elliott Bay to the location of today’s downtown, and the young town grew around timber, shipping and industry. After the Great Seattle Fire of 1889 destroyed much of downtown, the city rebuilt on a raised street grid, leaving behind the underground spaces that can still be explored today.

Seattle has reinvented itself repeatedly. The Klondike Gold Rush turned the city into a major outfitting and shipping center; World War II accelerated shipbuilding and aircraft manufacturing; the 1962 World’s Fair helped introduce the modern Seattle skyline; and technology companies later transformed the economy again. Yet the older layers never completely disappeared. Walk from Pike Place Market toward Pioneer Square, continue into the Chinatown-International District, head north to Ballard, or cross the Central District, and the city keeps revealing different versions of itself.
