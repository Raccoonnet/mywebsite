Washington

Washington is a state of contrasts that reveal themselves gradually as you travel. In the west, rain-darkened forests climb toward snow-capped mountains, ferries cross the waters of Puget Sound, and the Pacific Ocean shapes the rugged coastline. East of the Cascade Range, the scenery changes dramatically: orchards and vineyards spread across dry valleys, wheat fields roll toward the horizon, and towns sit beneath enormous skies. Between them is Seattle, a city whose identity has been shaped by water, mountains, industry, technology, and a long tradition of looking outward.

The landscape is inseparable from Washington’s history. Indigenous peoples have lived across these lands since long before the arrival of European settlers, developing cultures and communities closely connected to the region’s rivers, forests, mountains, and coastal waters. Later came maritime trade, timber, fishing, agriculture, railroads, and industry, followed in the modern era by aerospace, technology, life sciences, and global commerce. Washington became the 42nd state in 1889, but its story is much older than statehood.

Spend enough time here and you begin to notice how strongly residents respond to the landscape. A sunny summer afternoon can send people toward a trail, lake, farmers’ market, or waterfront almost instinctively, while the long gray season encourages bookstores, cafés, museums, music venues, and dinner tables. Washington isn’t one single experience. It is a collection of distinct regions connected by mountains, rivers, roads, ferries, and an unmistakable sense of place.
