Oregon

Oregon is a state of dramatic changes in scenery, often compressed into a single day’s drive. You can leave the wet evergreen forests of the Coast Range in the morning, pass through the broad farmland of the Willamette Valley, climb toward the snowfields of the Cascades, and finish beneath the open, sage-colored skies of Central or Eastern Oregon. The Pacific is never far from the story, but neither are rivers, volcanic peaks, high desert, working farms, and towns shaped by the landscape around them.

What makes Oregon especially interesting is the way geography has influenced everyday life. The Columbia and Willamette rivers helped Portland become a commercial center, while the Willamette Valley developed into one of the country’s important agricultural regions. Timber, fishing, farming, manufacturing, technology, wine, craft brewing, and outdoor industries all became part of the modern economy. Oregon’s history also reaches much further back than its American settlement: Indigenous peoples have lived throughout these landscapes for thousands of years, and their histories remain an essential part of understanding the state.

There is a particular rhythm to Oregon that visitors tend to notice after a few days. People linger over coffee, farmers’ markets become part of the weekly routine, mountain weather can alter a plan without apology, and a small coastal town may feel worlds apart from Portland even though both are unmistakably Oregon. The state rewards travelers who slow down enough to notice those differences.
